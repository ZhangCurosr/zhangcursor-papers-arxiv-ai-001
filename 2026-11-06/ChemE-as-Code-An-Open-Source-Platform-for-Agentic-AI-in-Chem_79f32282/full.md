# ChemE-as-Code: An Open-Source Platform for Agentic AI in Chemical Engineering

Artur M. Schweidtmann

Process Intelligence Research Group, Department of Chemical Engineering, Delft University of Technology, Van der Maasweg 9, 2629 HZ Delft, The Netherlands a.schweidtmann@tudelft.nl, ORCID 0000-0001-8885-6847

Preprint submitted to AIChE Journal, November 2026

## Abstract

ChemE-as-Code provides an open-source environment in which AI agents built on large language models (LLMs) can carry out chemical engineering work and engineers can check it. Its calculation tools range from catalysis through process simulation to plant layout, HAZOP and life cycle assessment, and skills tell a general-purpose agent how to use them. Engineering files are linked, machine-readable and versioned in Git; calculations run outside the LLM, each value records its origin, and automated checks report inconsistencies and out-of-date results by file and line. In an illustrative ammonia case, agents developed a written task into a preliminary basic engineering package (simulation files, P&IDs, layout, HAZOP, datasheets, relief) for USD 224 of API cost in the design sessions. Planted faults and engineering changes show that the checks find what their rules cover and miss the rest, so they guide human review rather than replace it. No independent engineer has reviewed the design.

Keywords: agentic AI, large language models, Plant-as-Code, process design, P&ID

## 1 Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence (AI) is expected to reshape chemical engineering [1, 2]. AI agents, its newest form, can go beyond answering questions: they can use tools, edit files and iterate on their own results [3, 4]. Engineering work can thus increasingly be proposed, executed and revised by computational agents. In the past years, software development has shown what this opens up. AI assistants have sped up well-defined programming tasks [5], and the length of tasks that agents complete on their own keeps growing [6]. Software teams can use agents in this way because their work already lives in versioned text files that automated tests can check. We expect chemical process engineering to benefit in the same way once its work takes the same form.

That form is harder to reach in chemical engineering than in software. A plant is described by many coupled documents: process models, specifications, process flow diagrams (PFDs), piping and instrumentation diagrams (P&IDs), equipment data, layouts, safety studies and environmental inventories. P&IDs are the central reference documents of a plant over its life cycle [7–9] and are still created largely by hand [7, 10]. The documents depend on each other, but the dependencies are rarely recorded. This means that a change in one place, such as a higher design capacity, silently invalidates numbers elsewhere: in the simulation, the drawings, the equipment data, the layout, the safety analysis and the inventory [11]. Nothing tells the engineer which of them. Much process information also still sits in documents that must be digitized before a change can be assessed [12]. Integrated information models, exchange standards and digital threads address parts of this problem [13–16]. They let tools exchange data. They do not record, for a whole project and at every revision, which results are still consistent with each other, which are out of date, and who has reviewed what.

AI agents make this problem larger. They produce more proposals and revisions than people can read, and their output can look plausible but still be invalid. Earlier generators of flowsheets and P&IDs, from rule-based systems [10, 17, 18] to learned models [19–21], have a related limit: they produce part of a design without the decisions behind it and outside the engineering workflow. As agents propose faster, verification increasingly sets the pace of engineering [22].

A further question is how agents should be organized. Our earlier review proposed teams of specialized agents, one per discipline, mirroring an engineering organization [4]. Here we take a diferent route. Domain knowledge sits in skills and tools, and one general-purpose agent loads what a task needs. A skill is a short instruction file that tells the agent how to perform an engineering task; a tool is a program that performs a calculation or a check. This keeps the engineering knowledge in files that engineers can read, test and version, independently of a particular agent or model, and it reduces the need for coordination between discipline-specific agents, a reported source of failure in multi-agent systems [23]. Separate sessions are still used where independence helps, as in review. Which option works better is an open question that needs a controlled comparison.

The acceleration that AI agents promise depends on the engineering environment in which they work. We present ChemE-as-Code, an open-source platform that provides such an environment. It implements the Plant-as-Code perspective of Schweidtmann and Demeyer [22] and extends it from process and plant design to process chemistry, simulation, techno-economic assessment (TEA) and life cycle assessment (LCA); the name stands for treating chemical engineering work as code. Early industrial pilots on individual projects have pointed to substantially faster design iterations [22], and ChemE-as-Code makes the approach open, broad and testable. The platform rests on two foundations. First, a plant is one Git repository of linked, machine-readable files, from the design basis (the requirements the plant must meet: product, capacity, feed, site and utilities) to the life cycle inventory. Every revision is checked by programs, and the checks and calculations behind each value are kept with it; we call this record the evidence of a revision. Second, an open set of calculation tools and agent skills spans the scales of chemical engineering, from catalysis and reaction kinetics to plant layout and LCA (Figure 1). Agents and engineers work through the same files and command line, and in the proposed workflow a person reviews the evidence before a revision is accepted. We argue that the innovation lies in this combination of capable agents with an engineering environment designed for them. Models will change quickly, but we expect the way agents interact with engineering files, tools, evidence and reviewers to remain relevant.

We ask three questions. RQ1 (representation): can a process project be represented as linked, machine-readable files across disciplines that agents and people edit? RQ2 (verification): do automated checks find inconsistencies, out-of-date results and afected files when the design changes, where do they fall short, and can generated files be regenerated reproducibly? RQ3 (agent-enabled engineering): can a general-purpose agent carry out interconnected engineering tasks with these skills and tools, at what cost and with which limitations? The paper contributes a paradigm for AI-accelerated, human-directed Chemical Engineering-as-Code, described as five shifts in how engineering work is organized (Section 3); the open-source platform that implements it (Section 4); and an end-to-end demonstration on a natural-gas ammonia plant with an evaluation of what the checks find and miss (Section 5). The case shows that the workflow runs. It does not establish the quality of the design or a productivity gain, which remains our hypothesis.

## 2 Related Work

ChemE-as-Code builds on decades of process systems engineering (PSE), engineering information management and software practice. What it adds is the combination: one versioned project in which agents make changes and programs check them for people. This section places that combination against four lines of prior work.

## 2.1 Process Engineering Information and Its Management

Simulation, flowsheeting, optimization and synthesis are the foundations of PSE [24], and connecting the information they produce is an old goal of the field [25]. Integrated models for data and documents [13] and the OntoCAPE ontology [14, 26] make process engineering knowledge machinereadable and reusable. Knowledge graphs now link process knowledge, models and the origin of data in digital twins [27–29]. Information technology for integrated process and plant design [30], the digitization of legacy drawings [12] and the digital thread in manufacturing [16] pursue the same aim. Model-based systems engineering and life cycle standards make traceability and configuration management explicit practice [31, 32], and design research has studied how a change to one component

propagates through a product’s structure [11].

In industrial practice, engineering document management systems (EDMS) control the revision, approval status and distribution of documents over a plant’s life cycle [33]. Data-centric engineering databases keep P&IDs, equipment and instrument lists in one store, so that the tags and connections on a drawing agree with the lists as long as both are edited in that tool [30, 34]. These systems solve part of the version and dependency problem. However, an EDMS tracks documents rather than their content: it records which revision of a P&ID is current, not whether that revision agrees with the instrument list or the simulation. Consistency in integrated tools ends at the tool boundary, and much of the information gathered in design does not reach operation in electronic and interoperable form [34].

## 2.2 Exchange Standards

P&ID data usually sit in proprietary formats of computer-aided engineering tools. DEXPI, based on ISO 15926 [35], defines a vendor-independent information model and XML exchange format for P&IDs and process models [15]. Version 2.0.1 covers block flow diagrams (BFDs), PFDs and P&IDs [36] and is the format ChemE-as-Code stores. pyDEXPI makes DEXPI data available to Python and to machine learning as graphs [37], so that DEXPI can act as the interface between generative models and industrial tools; graph learning on DEXPI plant topologies [38] and large language model (LLM) interaction with P&IDs [29, 39] build on it. CAPE-OPEN makes unit operations and property packages interoperable between process simulators [40], AutomationML exchanges engineering data in automation engineering [41], and building engineering has open, version-controlled common data environments such as Speckle [42]. However, standards solve exchange, not revision consistency. A valid DEXPI file says nothing about whether the equipment list, the simulation or the HAZOP study still agrees with it. This means that a standard can carry a design between tools, but it cannot tell an engineer what a change has invalidated.

## 2.3 AI in Process Engineering

AI in process engineering is starting to move from generators toward LLM agents, although these are still first works, and most methods address one document or one task at a time. The field reaches from expert systems, including knowledge-based P&ID configuration [10, 17, 18], through machine learning in PSE [43–45], to generative AI and LLMs [1, 2], which need to be grounded in domain knowledge [46]. For flowsheets and P&IDs, prior work has produced text representations [47–49], generative models that complete flowsheets and propose control structures [19, 20, 50, 51], and rule-based and learned methods that detect and correct errors [21, 52–54]. Open engineering data are scarce [55, 56], and generative models produce topologies without the attributes and decisions behind them, are scored against a single reference design although several designs can be valid, and are not integrated into engineering workflows. More recently, LLM agents carry out engineering tasks with tools, from process improvement, optimization and control [57–59] to P&ID generation [60, 61], simulation and model generation [62–66], conceptual separation design [4] and question answering on P&IDs [29, 39]. Most use task-specific tools with little human oversight [4]. Some recent work connects several tasks. Laub et al. digitize process descriptions into flowsheets and build converged Aspen Plus simulations, benchmarked against flowsheets from 50 experts [65]. Tian et al. go from textual specifications to executable process simulations with a multi-agent workflow [66], and Zhang et al. combine a knowledge graph with agents to assemble and calibrate process models for digita twins [28]. These works stop at flowsheets, simulations and process models; they do not manage and verify revisions across the further disciplines of a plant project. Beyond chemical engineering, tool-augmented agents plan and run chemistry experiments [3, 67], software-engineering agents work through agent–computer interfaces [68] and are evaluated on real repository issues [69], and multi-agent frameworks coordinate several LLM agents on one task [70, 71].

## 2.4 Engineering-as-Code and Reproducibility

Software engineering manages change through version control, continuous integration and delivery [72], and Infrastructure-as-Code applies the same practice to computing infrastructure [73]. Engineering-as-Code transfers it to engineering design [74]. In plant and automation engineering, industrial practice came first: Davy Demeyer’s Design-Ops work applied Engineering-as-Code and agentic practices before this work [22, 75], and early industrial pilots on individual projects point to substantially faster design iterations [22]. These pilots use closed software and their details are confidential, so their scope and results cannot be examined or reproduced. Plant-as-Code, proposed by Schweidtmann and Demeyer [22], provides a perspective on Engineering-as-Code for process plants.

Engineering results can only be trusted if a reader can see where a value came from and can regenerate it. Computational science has practices for this. Rules for reproducible research [76] and the FAIR principles [77] describe how to keep data and code findable and reusable, containers fix the software environment [78], and reproducible builds require that the same inputs yield outputs that are identical to the byte [79]. W3C PROV-O provides a vocabulary for recording which activity and which agent produced a result [80]. In engineering projects, by contrast, whether a value was given by a supplier, assumed by an engineer or calculated by a tool is often recorded only in documents, and a result often cannot be regenerated after a change.

Taken together, prior work provides rigorous models, shared semantics, portable formats, fast generation and the software practice for managing change. ChemE-as-Code brings them into one open project in which every revision is checked across disciplines and kept with its evidence, with tools and skills that let agents work across the scales of chemical engineering.

## 3 The Paradigm of AI-Accelerated Chemical Engineering-as-Code

Chemical Engineering-as-Code changes how engineering work is organized, not what engineers calculate. It extends Plant-as-Code beyond the plant to the whole discipline, including process chemistry, simulation and LCA. We describe this change as five shifts. Each has a counterpart in software development [68, 72], which we use as an analogy: what transfers is the way work is organized, not the nature of the product. Figure 2 shows one iteration of the resulting workflow for a capacity change, the example we use throughout: the plant is to make 20% more product, so the feed, the balances, the equipment, the drawings, the safety study and the inventory all have to follow.

(i) From documents to connected engineering knowledge. Today, a project is a set of deliverables, each made in its own tool and passed on as a document. In the new paradigm, the design basis, models, drawings, lists, layouts, safety studies and assessments form one connected body of machine-readable knowledge, together with the decisions and evidence behind them. The software analogy is source code and configuration kept as text in one repository, as in Infrastructureas-Code [73]. The diference is that drawings and documents do not disappear: they remain as views of the knowledge, issued when a stage requires them. Not every model has to become executable code, but the representations, decisions and dependencies that engineering work needs must be readable, changeable and checkable by people and programs alike. For the engineer, this means that a pump can be followed from the flowsheet to the P&ID, the equipment list and the HAZOP study, which an assistant bound to one tool cannot do [22].

(ii) From manual execution to human-directed agentic engineering. Engineers have so far carried out most engineering activities themselves, with tools for individual calculations. Now they have started to direct some workflows instead. They state objectives, constraints, specifications and acceptance criteria, and agents draft, calculate, revise and document, using deterministic engineering tools for every calculated number. The analogy is a developer who directs coding agents that open pull requests for review. The diference is where responsibility sits: in engineering, the engineer keeps the responsibility for the design, so an agent’s draft is a proposal until a person accepts it. Engineers can then spend more of their time on alternatives, critical decisions and review. If a first draft becomes cheap, a broader exploration of design alternatives may become afordable.

(iii) From isolated deliverables to continuously evolving projects. A deliverable is issued at the end of a phase and revised in the next. In Chemical Engineering-as-Code, people and agents change a shared project in small revisions. A revision is one recorded change with its reason, and it can span process, equipment, piping, control, safety and assessment at once. The history of the design, and why it took its present form, stays with the design. The analogy is small, reviewed commits with a recorded history instead of a release at the end of a development phase. The diference is that the engineering phases from concept to detailed engineering remain, with their formal releases and approvals; what can become much shorter are the iterations within them [22].

(iv) From periodic checking to continuous verification. Checks are traditionally tied to milestones such as design reviews and HAZOP studies. Agents, however, can propose revisions faster than anyone can check them [22], so milestone checks cannot keep up, and verification has to move into every iteration. A check is a program that tests one rule over the files, for example that every instrument on a P&ID is in the instrument list, and reports a finding with the file and line where the rule fails. Agents repair findings before a person looks at the revision, and every revision carries its evidence: the results of the checks and the calculations behind its values. The analogy is automated tests and continuous integration, which run on every commit. The speed of this loop depends on how cheap, local and trustworthy the checks are. Three states of work are kept apart. A proposed revision is what a person or agent has drafted. A checked revision has no error-level findings; its remaining warnings and the requirements that cannot yet be checked are stated with it. An approved revision has been accepted by a responsible engineer. The diference from software is firm: checks never replace approval, which is given by people, and a HAZOP study still needs a team.

(v) From discipline-specific automation to integrated engineering. Instead of automating a flowsheet, a P&ID or a HAZOP study one at a time, the aim is to connect these tasks, so that a change and its consequences are followed across disciplines and project stages. The capacity increase of Figure 2 then reaches the balances, the simulation, the drawings, the safety studies and the inventory in one revision, and the checks list which files the change afected, as far as the platform records the links between files. The analogy is one change made across several services of a software system in one repository and tested as a whole. The diference is that an engineering change crosses disciplines with their own tools, standards and responsible people, so the links between files have to be recorded explicitly.

When such projects are open and interoperable, engineering knowledge can accumulate. Designs, models, decisions and evidence that are today rebuilt within each organization could be inspected, reused and extended by others, and work at scales that were impractical, such as broad searches over design alternatives or the reassessment of many designs when a shared assumption changes, could become possible (Section 6). These shifts do not depend on a particular technology. Version control, plain-text formats, exchange standards such as DEXPI and today’s agents are implementation choices. The paradigm is that engineering knowledge is accessible to computational agents in a shared environment where every action is traceable, every result can be checked, and people decide.

## 4 The ChemE-as-Code Platform

ChemE-as-Code combines a shared plant repository with calculation tools, skills for agents and automated checks. Table 1 defines the six terms that the rest of the paper relies on.

The platform consists of three open repositories. The core is the toolkit: its cheme command validates, formats, renders, compares and generates engineering files, and it includes the skills that instruct agents and a browser viewer (cheme serve). The project template is the starting layout for a new plant repository. The process library holds complete plants, including the case study of this paper. A plant is one Git repository of plain files for every discipline, from the design basis through process, equipment, piping, control, safety and layout to cost and the life cycle inventory (LCI), which engineers and agents edit and the core reads, checks and extends (Figure 3). This means that an agent sees the whole plant at once, as a coding agent sees a whole software repository rather than one file [68, 69]. A change across disciplines is one revision, reviewed as one dif and reverted as a whole, and the checks compare files of the same revision with each other. Large software organizations keep most of their code in a single repository for related reasons, namely unified versioning and atomic changes across projects [81]. At version 1.1.0 the core comprises about 143,000 lines of Python with about 3,000 test functions. SI Section S2 gives its detailed architecture, and SI Section S4 lists its tools and skills by scale.

## 4.1 Engineering Files in Git

Git keeps versions of files and records who changed what, when and why. It does not know what the files mean. Git does not distinguish a pump from a pipe, and it merges text without regard to engineering meaning, so two revisions that are each consistent can merge into one that is not. Suppose one revision renames pump P-301A to P-303A in the equipment list, and another, made in parallel, adds a line to P-301A on the P&ID. Git merges both without complaint, and the merged state names a pump on the P&ID that the list no longer has. ChemE-as-Code adds the engineering meaning that Git lacks. Each file declares its kind and the schema it follows, items refer to each other by tag (the number of a piece of equipment or a line, such as H-101 or NG-1003), and the checks run on every proposed revision in continuous integration. In the example, cheme validate reports that the P&ID refers to a tag that is not in the equipment list, with the file and the line, before the revision can be accepted.

The following standards and formats are used. P&IDs are stored in DEXPI 2.0.1 [15], based on ISO 15926, and handled with pyDEXPI [37]; the BFD and PFD are generated as DEXPI process models from the flowsheet. All other engineering information is held in YAML files, each validated against a versioned JSON Schema that the file declares; the declaration is added to older files by cheme migrate, the mechanism through which files will move to later schema versions. Decisions are kept in a decision log (DESIGN.md), reviews as Markdown records, and stage sign-ofs in a generated status file. Three properties make these files suitable for agents and for review. They are canonical: cheme fmt writes DEXPI XML with a readable identifier on every object, so that a P&ID sheet of tens of thousands of lines still yields a small, reviewable dif. They are linked: list items refer to P&ID objects by their tags, flowsheet nodes link to equipment and sheets, and HAZOP studies record the revision of the sheets they analyzed. Relations are recorded only where a check needs them, not in a universal object model, so an ontology, a knowledge graph or an EDMS can be built from a plant repository. They are typed: every YAML kind has a schema, so a wrong unit or a missing field is a finding, not a surprise downstream.

The files at one commit are the engineering state of the plant and its source of truth. A person or an agent changes it by proposing a revision, normally on a branch, which becomes the next state once accepted in review and merged. In the proposed workflow, acceptance is a human decision, and the change is merged by the reviewer, not by its author; the platform does not enforce this yet, but branch protection on the Git host can. In the case study (Section 5), a reviewer agent accepted the changes, so the design is agent-generated, with the author’s recorded interventions and no independent human review. Such an acceptance lets a change merge; it is not the approval of a responsible engineer.

Every file is either authoritative or derived. Authoritative files are written by people or agents: the design basis, given and assumed values, P&IDs, lists, layout inputs, HAZOP records, decisions and reviews. Derived files are generated by a tool: the BFD and PFD from the flowsheet, simulation runs and calculated values, the 3D plant model, the material take-of (MTO), estimates and LCI datasets. Derived files are never edited by hand. Where implemented, they record a fingerprint of their inputs and the version of the tool that generated them, and cheme validate reports a file or value whose inputs no longer match as out of date, as it reports a hand edit of a generated sheet. A calculated value never overwrites a given or assumed one: a tool writes only the values it calculated itself, so if a given flow no longer closes a balance, a check reports the imbalance instead of a tool correcting the input. Nothing is regenerated, rewritten or declared invalid automatically. A derived file stays as it is, marked out of date, until a person or agent runs the command that regenerates it, and regenerating it with the same inputs and the same tool version gives output that is identical to the byte (SI Section S4).

## 4.2 Tools and Skills for Agents

The core separates what agents propose from what tools compute. Its engineering domains (chemistry, process, equipment, piping, control, safety, spatial and assessment) contain no calls to LLMs. Calculated values enter the files only through deterministic commands or recorded scripts; in the case study, the agents wrote some of these scripts themselves, which Section 6 discusses. Established programs do the heavy calculations: DWSIM solves the flowsheet, thermo, chemicals and CoolProp supply physical properties, pyDEXPI reads and writes P&IDs, and Brightway computes the LCA [82]. The core’s own tools cover what sits between them: balances and propagation, shortcut sizing of exchangers, pumps, compressors and columns, the 3D model with pipe routing and clash detection, HAZOP rating, cost estimation from published correlations, and TEA. SI Section S4 lists them by scale.

The agent’s interface is the command line and the files themselves, the same interface a software engineer uses. The loop is simple. The agent edits a file, runs cheme fmt and cheme validate, reads the findings, fixes what they report, and validates again. Each finding is one line with a stable identifier, the rule, a severity, the file and, where one applies, the line, and a hint. An agent can therefore act on a finding as it would on a failing test. The engineer sees the same lines in continuous integration and in the viewer.

Skills carry the engineering procedure. A skill is a short instruction file in the plant repository that tells the agent which inputs a task needs, how to carry it out, which tools to call, and which decisions are left to people. The 109 skills of version 1.1.0 span the scales of a project, from component properties and reaction kinetics through thermodynamic-model selection, flowsheet simulation and reactor screening to equipment sizing, P&ID authoring, control, 3D layout, HAZOP, cost estimation, TEA and LCA. About half of them are process knowledge: unit operations, utilities, heat integration, and the technologies of gas processing, refining, petrochemicals and heavy chemicals.

The skill for selecting a thermodynamic model shows the division of labor between skill, tool and person. It is used before any phase calculation and before a property method is recorded in the materials file. The skill tells the agent to establish the facts first: the pressure range, whether the mixture is polar, whether electrolytes are present, whether interaction parameters are available, and whether a second liquid phase or vapor association is expected. The agent fills in only what the given information or well-established chemistry supports and leaves the rest unset. The tool behind the skill is a rule engine that implements Carlson’s published decision tree. If a fact it needs is missing, it refuses and names the missing fact as a question, which the agent answers or escalates. The result is a recommendation with its reasoning, recorded with provenance, and the skill states that it is not a validated choice. In the ammonia case, this skill recommended a predictive equation of state or a cubic equation of state with Huron–Vidal mixing rules for the polar loop mixture at 155 bar. The simulation route could not run either, so the agents used Peng–Robinson as a substitute and recorded the decision, and the calculation maturity later reported these mixtures as outside the benchmarked range of the method (SI Section S8). The HAZOP skill works in the same way: it drafts nodes, deviations and safeguards, the rating tool looks up the risk band in the matrix, and every entry stays marked as proposed until a HAZOP team accepts it.

This division means that engineering expertise becomes accessible to agents without being embedded in an LLM. The numbers come from tools that can be tested and versioned. The procedures come from skills that engineers can read, review and improve like any other file. The decisions stay with people.

## 4.3 Checks, Gates and Human Approval

Agents produce more revisions than an engineer can read, so the checks tell the engineer where to look and what not to trust yet. Each check tests one rule that a program can decide, in four layers [22]: every file matches its schema (a design temperature is a number with a unit); files agree with each other (a line’s diameter in the line list matches the P&ID that draws it); balances and simulations hold (every mass balance closes within its tolerance); and selected safety requirements are met (a trip does not share its transmitter with the control loop it protects against). The core defines 466 rules (SI Section S3), and every finding names the file and, where one applies, the line.

A design change shows how the checks work together. Suppose an agent raises the design capacity by 20% and the feed flows with it. The balance rules report that the flowsheet no longer closes; every calculated result whose input dependencies are recorded, from stream properties to the simulation run, is reported as out of date through its fingerprint; and cheme impact lists the files and reviews that need attention. Nothing is recalculated on its own. The agent or the engineer reruns the relevant tools and checks whether the afected results are now current, and the reviews that the change made out of date are redone. This sequence (check, identify the afected work, recalculate, review) follows only the dependencies the platform records; a duty copied by hand from the flowsheet into a datasheet is not among them (Section 5.5).

The checks support the decision whether work can proceed to the next project stage; they do not take it. Each stage has gates (Table 1), for example that every item on the equipment list has a datasheet marked final, and a stage is signed of only when its gates pass and no serious review finding is open (SI Section S3). The platform also reports calculation maturity, a programevaluated summary of which calculation requirements have been met (SI Section S3). Passing these requirements establishes only that the conditions encoded in them have been met, not that the design is right or that an engineer has approved it. Approval is reserved for people in the proposed workflow; the core records reviews but does not enforce this, and in the case study it accepted sign-ofs on agent reviews.

## 5 Ammonia Case Study

We demonstrate the platform on a natural-gas ammonia plant, following the design from its initial requirements through simulation, engineering documents and safety studies. We chose ammonia because its design couples many disciplines, from reaction equilibria and recycle convergence through equipment, piping, instrumentation and layout to cost estimation. We ask whether agents can use the platform to develop a connected engineering project (RQ1, RQ3), how far the checks identify errors and incomplete changes, whether derived files can be regenerated (RQ2), and whether an agent can revise the plant through the same workflow (RQ2, RQ3). Three kinds of success have to be kept apart: execution, meaning that agents generated and modified a multidisciplinary project; formal verification, meaning that the implemented rules and gates checked the conditions they cover; and engineering correctness, which this case does not establish, because no independent engineer has reviewed the design.

## 5.1 Setup

Agents developed the plant in two stages (Table 2): a concept stage of three sessions that produced the design basis, a balanced flowsheet and the equipment list, and an engineering stage that started from a written brief by the author (SI Section S5). In the engineering stage, an orchestrating session ran one implementation job per section, each followed by a reviewer job in a separate session that returned the work for correction or merged it once continuous integration had passed. These are workflow roles of one general-purpose agent, not discipline-specific agents. Unlike in the proposed workflow, reviewer agents accepted the changes, so no engineer’s acceptance enters the record. The author’s interventions were the brief, the budget and stop rules, and the decisions the agents escalated (SI Section S9). The evaluations of Section 5.5 ran on the reported state, process-library tag nh3-run-final, where every file of the case is public.

## 5.2 The Engineering Package

The engineering stage turned the concept-stage flowsheet into a connected package: a converged simulation, P&IDs with their lists, control loops and datasheets, a routed 3D layout, a HAZOP study, relief cases, a class 4 estimate with TEA, and an LCI, in one repository whose files refer to each other by tag (Table 2). It passed every concept- and basic-stage gate and three of the five detail gates with no error-level finding. Figure 4 follows one piece of equipment, the reformer furnace H-101, through these files: the 3D model, the P&ID, the HAZOP study and the links between them. Rules check that its tag refers to the same item in the flowsheet, the equipment list, the P&ID, the datasheet, the layout and the HAZOP study, and the HAZOP study records the revision of the sheet it analyzed, so a later change to the sheet marks the study as needing review. The figure also shows a limit of these links. The datasheet and the cost basis state a duty of 68,616 kW, whereas the flowsheet changed later and now gives 69,829 kW; no rule compares the two, so no finding reports the diference.

The techno-economic assessment gives a levelized cost of 688 EUR/t against 439 EUR/t assumed for imported ammonia. Under its assumptions, 2025 Dutch industrial energy prices and a US Gulf Coast import price without freight (SI Section S7), the plant would not compete with imports. The

LCI records every exchange of the flowsheet per kg of ammonia and gives a gate-to-gate carbon footprint of 1.46 kg CO<sub>2</sub>-eq per kg, with 6% of the burden allocated to the exported steam by exergy. This counts only the plant’s own emissions: the background datasets for natural gas supply, electricity and utilities are deliberately left unlinked as open gaps, so the figure is a screening result, not a cradle-to-gate footprint.

The package is preliminary, not an engineering-approved basic design. The basic gates passed only after the author accepted two layout exceptions that the agents had escalated rather than decided (SI Section S9), the stage sign-ofs rest on agent review records, and review findings remain open. The calculation maturity reaches balanced, not predictive: several reactors and separators use specified conversions and splits, the furnace, refrigeration and steam system are evaluated by agent-written scripts on simulated duties, and the property method has not been benchmarked for most of the mixtures (SI Sections S7 and S8). Three of the eight relief valves still lack a required capacity, and every HAZOP entry is an agent’s proposal, because no HAZOP team has met.

Reviewer agents recorded 2 high, 39 medium and 52 low findings in 26 review sessions, and 13 times over 7 review rounds they sent work back for fixing. The findings included engineering defects as well as formal ones: exchangers with a large pressure step between their sides had no tube-rupture case, and heat-transfer coeficients for gases near atmospheric pressure had been used for the 150 bar loop. As the brief required, eight jobs stopped and reported a blocker instead of relaxing a gate. One job, however, marked datasheets final so that a gate would count them, and a reviewer caught this. The run also found eight defects in the platform software, of which three were fixed by agent jobs outside the design (SI Section S9). These reviewers use the same model as the designers and are therefore not independent [22].

## 5.3 Cost and Efort

The recorded run used 63 agent sessions at an API cost of USD 255.31 (Table 3): USD 12.02 for the concept stage (3 sessions, 40 minutes) and USD 243.30 for the engineering stage (60 sessions, about 27 hours). Layout and its downstream sections took 40% of the design cost and 26 of the 56 design sessions, because they needed the most iteration. Review and fix jobs took about a quarter of the design cost, and nearly half when the close-out jobs (USD 44), which are booked as implementation, are included. These figures describe one execution with a particular model and heavy caching (565 million cached input tokens), on a first-generation toolchain and with a workflow nobody had practiced before. They bound neither the best nor the typical case, and a more mature platform and more practiced briefs will change them. An order-of-magnitude estimate of the inference energy from the recorded tokens and published per-token figures [83, 84] is given in SI Section S9. The cost figures exclude the orchestrating session, whose own API cost was not recorded, as well as human expertise, infrastructure and independent review. They are not evidence of productivity compared with an engineering team.

## 5.4 Plausibility Against Open References

To obtain an outside reference, we compared eight of ten planned indicators with the EU reference document on best available techniques (BAT) for ammonia (BREF) [85] and the International Energy Agency (IEA) ammonia roadmap [86] (Table 4; the two NOx indicators are not modeled, and the net energy appears twice, as delivered and with electricity at an assumed 40% conversion). The reference values were written before the engineering stage and withheld from the design jobs. Overall, the design appears plausible: process $\mathrm { C O } _ { 2 } .$ , loop pressure and conversion per pass lie inside the published ranges, and the energy indicators lie close to them. The comparison is only an indication, for three reasons. First, the references leave their boundaries and conversion factors partly open. The net specific energy is 26.97 GJ/t with electricity counted as delivered and 30.1 GJ/t at an assumed conversion eficiency of 40%, against a BAT range of 27.6–31.8 GJ/t (±1.5 GJ), and the comparison cannot establish whether the design is more or less eficient than a BAT plant. Second, the design imports more electricity than the IEA’s reference plant (2.1 against 0.3 GJ/t), because all its machines are electric, which lowers its natural-gas figure, and its lower steam export is not a merit, since its furnace, steam and refrigeration models are simplified. Third, the comparison was not fully blind, because a design agent opened the BREF during the run. We found no design value that moved toward the reference figures, but we cannot exclude influence (SI Section S11).

## 5.5 What the Checks Find and Miss

Three experiments test the checks and the regeneration of derived files on fresh copies of the reported state (cheme 1.1.0, DWSIM 9.0.5), and a fourth asks an agent to change the plant. Unmodified, the state gives 0 errors and 268 warnings and passes 23 of 25 gates. Table 5 summarizes the results; SI Sections S12 and S13 list every fault and change.

Planted faults. To see what the checks find, we injected one fault at a time into a copy of the files and ran the checks. We first wrote 19 faults knowing the rules, aimed at schema, consistency, physical, out-of-date and review rules, such as a line enlarged in the line list but not on the P&ID, or a feed temperature changed without rerunning the simulation. All 19 triggered their intended rule, with a finding at the edited file in 12 cases and at the edited line in 10. This confirms that the rules fire on the faults they target, but it says nothing about faults nobody wrote a rule for. We therefore had a separate agent session write 12 further faults on a copy of the engineering files from which the checks, skills and reviews had been removed, so that it could not see the rules. We count a fault as identified when a new finding names the wrong condition itself; a finding that only says that a file changed or that a result is out of date does not count. By this definition, the checks identified 4 of the 12: a mistyped relief-valve tag, a vessel placed on top of another, a hand-edited HAZOP risk band and a trip sharing its transmitter with the control loop. Five raised only a generic change signal, among them a relief valve set above its vessel’s design pressure and plain carbon steel chosen for $\mathrm { a - 3 3 ^ { \circ } C }$ drum. Three raised nothing, including a let-down valve changed to fail open. Four further planted values that were implausible but self-consistent, such as a datasheet pressure of by a factor of ten, also raised only change signals. This means that the checks find what contradicts an implemented rule, but not an error that no rule covers, even when another file states the conflicting value.

Engineering changes. To see how far the checks reach, we made five engineering changes on separate copies, from a re-rated pump to a 20% capacity increase, and ran the checks once after each. Beforehand, a separate agent session had listed, for each change, the files it expected to be afected and whether each needed an edit, a regeneration or a review; no engineer has checked these lists, so the numbers are indicative. The checks flagged or named 13 of 22 expected files for a re-tagged pump and 5 of 161 for the capacity increase, and fewer than half for the other three changes. After the capacity increase, the checks raised balance errors, marked the simulation and 105 stream values as out of date, lowered the calculation maturity and refused the PFD rebuild, but they did not flag most downstream disciplines, because the checks stop at the first broken layer until it is repaired. This means that one pass of the checks shows the engineer where to start, not everything a change will touch.

Reproducibility. We regenerated derived files with fixed software versions. The six generators tested (BFD and PFD, plant model, one P&ID rendering, two datasheet drawings, LCA, MTO), two DWSIM runs and library builds from one commit gave output identical to the byte [79]. After a feed-temperature change, a 72 s simulation rerun cleared all 106 out-of-date findings. On another operating system, the BFD, PFD, plant model and the two datasheet drawings regenerated identically, whereas the LCA and MTO files difered in their line endings, a defect in the commands that write them. The derived files are therefore reproducible with fixed software versions, apart from these line endings.

Agent-driven change. Finally, one unattended agent session was asked to raise the capacity to

1,800 t/d and propagate the change as far as it could on a budget described as tight, resolving what the checks report and listing the equipment it judged must be resized, without relaxing a gate or signing of (SI Section S13). In 49 minutes and for USD 8.55 of a USD 19.90 cap, it raised the design capacity and the feed flows, re-simulated the plant to 1,799 t/d with closed balances, and updated control setpoints, datasheet duties, relief loads, the estimate and the LCA; it changed 128 of the 161 files on the expected-impact list. A separate rerun of the checks found 0 errors and 252 warnings, a calculation maturity of balanced, and four failing gates: the two detail gates that had failed before and two reviews that the change had made out of date. Yet the plant was not resized. The flows and duties are 20% larger on paper, but no P&ID or line changed, the equipment dimensions and the layout still describe the 1,500 t/d plant, and 36 lines exceed their design velocities by the agent’s own screening, not by any check, because no rule compares a flow with a line size. The agent reported this and stated that it would not trust its costs. The tight budget may have contributed, so this result does not measure the most that agents can do. The outcome is mixed: an agent carried out substantial cross-disciplinary work through the shared repository, but the checks follow only the dependencies the platform records, so zero errors did not mean that the change was complete, and a reviewer was still needed.

## 6 Discussion and Future Perspectives

We return to the problem of Section 1: agents produce engineering work faster than people can check it. The case shows one way to organize that work and where it still falls short.

AI acceleration and the practice of engineering. In the proposed workflow, agents act on findings, people review a dif with its evidence, and progress is judged by the evidence instead of the number of drawings. We argue that this is the precondition for acceleration: without it, faster drafting only produces more unchecked documents. How much of the acceleration survives review remains unclear. The length of software tasks that agents complete on their own has doubled about every seven months since 2019 [6], and most technology professionals report that AI has increased their productivity [87], but such gains depend on the task, and this study does not establish a net time saving once engineering review is counted. In the ammonia case, a package that passed the basic-stage gates cost USD 224 in API calls for its design sessions, but its reviewers sent work back 13 times, the last gates needed decisions from a person, and an independent engineer would still have to review the result. We expect the cost of first drafts to fall much faster than the cost of checking and review, which would then set the pace and change what teams must build and education must teach [22]. Controlled studies against engineering baselines, comparable to SWE-bench for software agents [69], are in preparation for process design, HAZOP and other tasks.

Known and novel processes. Ammonia from natural gas is among the best-documented processes in chemical engineering, and the route was set in the first task. The case therefore tests whether agents can carry a known process through engineering work consistently, not whether they can conceive a new one. We believe that particular care is needed with novel processes, for three reasons. LLMs draw on published knowledge and may fall back on familiar flowsheets where a diferent one would be better. New processes lack the kinetic, property and cost data on which the deterministic tools depend, so more of the design rests on assumptions. And the rules and gates encode established practice: they can show that an unconventional design is inconsistent, not that it is good, and they may flag a sound new idea as an error. Cheap and traceable iterations may still help creative work by letting many more alternatives be generated and screened, with people judging which are worth pursuing; testing this needs design tasks on processes published after the model’s knowledge cutof, or hypothetical routes judged by experts.

Consistent is not correct. Keeping calculations outside the LLM makes them repeatable and testable, but a deterministic calculation with a wrong assumption, an unsuitable property method or an incomplete model gives the same wrong result every time. The ammonia case illustrated this in part: the energy indicators looked favorable until their boundaries were examined, a wrong design value that no rule covers raised no finding that named it, and a capacity change raised no errors although the plant had not been resized. The agents also wrote some of the calculation scripts themselves. This means that the checks establish consistency within the dependencies the platform records, and an engineer must still judge the assumptions, the models and what the rules do not cover. The missed faults point to rules that compare values that today only change together, such as a datasheet duty against the flowsheet; a rule is a small piece of deterministic code, and benchmarks with planted faults on open plants would show how far the coverage has come.

An open ecosystem. Much engineering knowledge sits in proprietary project files, vendor-specific tools and internal databases. ChemE-as-Code keeps it in open, documented formats, so a project remains readable without any single application, tools and skills from diferent organizations can work on the same files, and commercial systems, whose scope and certification open tools do not replace, can read and write them through DEXPI and CAPE-OPEN [15, 40]. Open projects matter most. Complete, machine-readable plants with their models, drawings, decisions, safety studies and inventories have rarely been openly available [55, 56]. They could serve as benchmarks, teaching material and shared expertise [22], and because their assumptions and open points are public, engineers can review them by raising issues and proposing changes. Life cycle inventories derived from such projects can be recalculated from their models, which established databases such as ecoinvent [88] do not expose. The Process Atlas, an open database of process models and LCIs generated from the process library, is a forthcoming application [89]; its breadth and data quality are not results of this paper.

Risks. Three risks follow directly from this work [22]. The first is over-trust. In the ammonia case, a capacity change produced zero errors although the plant had not been resized; an engineer who reads a clean check as a complete design is misled. The platform reports calculation maturity and open warnings next to the errors and records who reviewed what, but the habit of reading the evidence rather than the status has to be built. The second is liability and certification. When an agent drafts a design and an agent accepts it, no person has taken responsibility for it, and current codes assume that one has. Rules are needed on who approves an agent-made design, which record of the agent’s work an approval must cite, and which checks a certifying body accepts as evidence; the run records, review records and sign-of rules of this platform are a starting point, not a substitute. The third is the training of engineers. Junior engineers learn much of their craft through the routine checking and data transfer that agents now take over, and the profession could lose the judgment that review needs unless this experience is built in new ways, for example by having junior engineers review and challenge agent work together with its evidence. An error in a shared skill, rule or template could also spread to many designs at once, and machine-readable plant models raise questions of cyber security and intellectual property, which we note without treating them here.

Limitations. The case study is illustrative and agent-generated, with the author’s recorded interventions. Its reviewers are agents of the same model as its designers, so their findings show what the model catches in its own work, not what an engineer would catch. The checks establish consistency within the rules that exist; engineering judgment and plausibility, mechanical design, relief sizing, dynamics, the correctness of a HAZOP rating and dependencies not yet fingerprinted, such as datasheet duties, lie outside them, which is why the held-out faults and the capacity change found what they did. The case uses one general-purpose agent in workflow roles and ofers no controlled comparison with other agent architectures or with the same agent without skills, the comparison that would show what the skills contribute. Proprietary tools hold most industrial data, DEXPI coverage is incomplete, and agents are nondeterministic, so two runs of the same brief give diferent designs. ChemE-as-Code is a first open research prototype, and these limits should be read in that context. The same pattern could extend to the operation of existing plants, to laboratory work where experiments and synthesis procedures are already written as code [90, 91], and to molecular and product design; the core is layered by domain so that such domains can be added.

## 7 Conclusions

AI agents ofer the prospect of substantially accelerating chemical process engineering and reducing its cost, but realizing this requires more than capable models. We argue that engineering knowledge must be connected, machine-readable and checkable, so that agents can work across disciplines while engineers keep responsibility for decisions and approval.

ChemE-as-Code implements this through two foundations: a versioned repository of linked engineering files, and an open library of deterministic tools and agent skills that spans chemica engineering from catalysis and reaction kinetics to plant layout and LCA. A general-purpose agent uses these across disciplines, while calculations, checks and approval stay outside the LLM, with approval reserved for people in the proposed workflow. In an illustrative natural-gas ammonia case, agent sessions turned a written brief into a preliminary package that passed the platform’s basic-stage gates, with HAZOP, datasheets and relief cases, for USD 224 of API cost in the design sessions, and a comparison with open references indicates that the design is plausible. The checks caught every fault written with knowledge of the rules but only some of the faults written without them, and a capacity change raised no errors although the plant had not been resized. An error-free result therefore shows consistency, not a complete or correct design. The case shows that the workflow runs. It does not establish the quality of the design or a productivity gain, and no independent engineer has reviewed the result.

Beyond automation, ChemE-as-Code lays a foundation for an open chemical engineering ecosystem in which process models, engineering data, decisions and the evidence behind them are reusable assets rather than remaining inside single tools and projects. The longer-term opportunity is to change how engineering knowledge is created, checked, shared and reused, first in process and plant design and then across chemical engineering.

## S3 Platform: Rules, Calculation Maturity, Gates and Sign-ofs

Rules. The checks follow the four rule layers of Schweidtmann and Demeyer (schema, consistency, physical, safety). At core 1.1.0 the code defines 466 rule identifiers: 390 written out in the source and 76 built from a prefix and a sufix (for example cheme.layout-<rule>); there is no central rule registry, so the count is by identifier. Every finding carries a stable identifier, a severity (error, warning, info), the file, the line where one applies, a message and a hint. The checks stop at the first broken layer until it is repaired, for example at a balance that does not close, which is why one pass of the checks after a large change reaches only part of the afected files, as the engineering-change experiments of the main text show.

Calculation maturity. cheme maturity is a checklist evaluated by a program, not a judgment. It rates how far the numbers of a design are backed by evidence in the repository on four cumulative levels, sourced, balanced, predictive and engineering, whose requirements Table S1 lists. A requirement the command cannot check blocks the level, so a level is claimed only when its requirements have been checked.

The architecture figure of the main text shows the simplified version.

Gates and sign-ofs. A gate is a set of checks that a project stage must pass, for example that every item on the equipment list has a datasheet marked final; core 1.1.0 defines 7 concept, 13 basic and 5 detail gates. A stage is signed of only on a clean, committed state with all its gates passing and no open critical or high review finding. Review records name their reviewer kind: human, agent or self, where self marks a review by the same session that made the change. The sign-of command refuses a sign-of on a self review unless someone else has approved it, and a sign-of counts only once it is merged, so an agent’s review is never mistaken for an engineer’s. A revision counts as checked when it is valid (every file matches its schema), consistent (no cross-file or physical rule reports an error) and current (no derived result is out of date); warnings, completeness and calculation maturity are reported with it, so this is not an overall pass or fail. Passing gates does not constitute a review. Approval and independent review are reserved for people in the proposed workflow; the core does not enforce this, and in the case study it accepted sign-ofs on agent reviews.

## S8 Ammonia: Model Basis and Modeling Assumptions

This section lists the main modeling assumptions and known limitations of the design. Agents made these choices and recorded them in DESIGN.md, whose decisions the D-numbers refer to; no engineer has reviewed them. The full record, including the reasoning behind each decision, is at https:// github.com/ChemE-as-Code/process-library/tree/nh3-run-final/processes/ammonia-1. In process/flowsheet.yaml, 20 values are given with a source, 135 are assumed with a reason and 268 are calculated.

Requirements that are not met. Table S5 lists the requirements of cheme maturity that the design does not pass.

Property method (D-025). The simulated plant uses Peng–Robinson with DWSIM’s own $k _ { i j }$ . The model-selection skill recommended PSRK or a cubic equation of state with modified Huron–Vidal mixing. The simulation route runs neither, so the agent used Peng–Robinson as a substitute and recorded the substitution for the reviewer. This loses the mixing rule for the polar components, and no $k _ { i j }$ is sourced for $\mathrm { N H _ { 3 } }$ with $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } , N _ { 2 } , C H _ { 4 } }$ or $\operatorname { A r } ,$ or for $\mathrm { C O _ { 2 } \mathrm { - H _ { 2 } O } }$ . The main weak spots are therefore the condensation of $\mathrm { N H _ { 3 } }$ with dissolved gases at the loop separator (263 K, 147 bar) and the $\mathrm { C O _ { 2 } }$ solubility in the process condensate. The method has not been benchmarked for these mixtures (Table S5), and no error estimate exists. The aMDEA $\mathrm { C O _ { 2 } }$ removal stays a specified separator, because it would need an electrolyte model and rate-based absorption, which the simulation route does not support.

Other assumptions and omissions. The feeds use a published representative gas and air composition approved by the author (D-040), and the sulfur specification stays assumed (D-004). Ambient conditions are the ISO 13443 reference conditions (288.15 K, 101.325 kPa, dry air), not climate data for the site (D-040), and cooling water runs from 298 to 308 K (D-012). Only the design case is calculated (D-003). The loop is very sensitive to the process air (D-040). With $6 8 , 6 6 0 \ \mathrm { k g / h , H _ { 2 } / N _ { 2 } }$ at the converter inlet is 2.994; 0.15% more air (68,760 kg/h) lowers it to 2.72, and 0.9% more (69,250 $\mathrm { { k g / h } ) }$ to 1.80 with the loop flow up by a third. Nitrogen fed above the stoichiometric ratio can leave the loop only with the purge, about 0.6% of the separator gas, and the purge-gas recovery returns a quarter of it (D-013), so a small excess accumulates in the loop. The hydrodesulfurization hydrogen recycle, dryer regeneration, condensate stripper and by-product methanol are not modeled. Furnace NOx, CO, $\mathrm { C H _ { 4 } }$ and $\mathrm { N _ { 2 } O }$ , consumables and fugitive emissions are not estimated (DESIGN.md, Section 6). Estimate of inference energy. The ledger at nh3-run-final records, for the 63 sessions of both stages, 3.16 million output tokens, 14.7 million cache-write tokens, 565 million cache-read tokens and 7,618 uncached input tokens. The provider publishes no energy data for the model and its size is not public, so this estimate is an order of magnitude only. For output tokens we scale the median of 0.31 Wh for a 300-token query on models above 200 billion parameters, which includes node overhead and data-center overhead (Oviedo et al., Joule, 2026, doi:10.1016/j.joule.2026.102430), giving 1.03 Wh per 1,000 tokens and 3.3 kWh. Their long-query value (3.91 Wh for 5,000 tokens) gives 2.5 kWh. Their model excludes long-context management, which dominates token counts in agent sessions. For input tokens we use 449 mJ per token for a 120-billion-parameter model, the largest reported by Vartziotis et al. (arXiv:2607.26571). This is an analytical, GPU-only value, which we multiply by the data-center overhead of 1.56 they quote. Cache writes then give 2.9 kWh, so the lower case, with cache reads taken as negligible, is about 6 kWh. The upper case charges every cache read as a full input token (110 kWh) and gives about 116 kWh. We consider this pessimistic because a cache read avoids recomputing the prompt, but the true cost of a read is not published, and a larger model would raise both cases. The range of 6 to 116 kWh is wide because the energy of a cached token is unknown; for comparison, even the upper value equals about 11 s of the designed plant’s 36.8 MW electricity import. The estimate covers inference only: it excludes the orchestration of both stages, local tools, the simulator, continuous integration and model training.

## S11 Ammonia: Comparison with Open References

Method. The reference values were written after the concept stage and before the engineering stage to benchmarks/ammonia.yaml on branch benchmark/ammonia (commit 7651243, 29 October 2026, 09:18 CET), which no design job checked out; no job prompt contained them. After the basic signof, a separate job computed the indicators from the design’s files (runs/benchmark-kpis.md, reproduced by scripts/benchmark\_kpis.py). Bases: per t of ammonia product (62,472 kg/h); lower heating values from the formation enthalpies of process/materials.yaml (natural gas 49.09 MJ/kg); electricity as delivered energy (36.77 MW, 3.6 GJ/MWh); exported MP steam at its enthalpy above the make-up water (3.10 MJ/kg). With the steam credited at its exergy instead, the net specific energy is 28.84 GJ/t. Two indicators (process and total direct $\mathrm { C O _ { 2 } ) }$ are bound to the LCI dataset and checked in every library build; the energy indicators cannot be expressed over the dataset’s mass-based exchanges (process-library issue 272).

Reference definitions. BREF Table 2.14 gives 27.6–31.8 GJ(LHV)/t, 1.5 GJ, as net energy consumption for BAT. It prescribes no formula, electricity factor or steam credit, and Section 2.3.1.1 says that a fair comparison needs the balance with the conversion factors used. Table 2.6 notes that net energy depends on the conversion factor for imported electricity. The range 29.3–31.1 GJ/t is our sum of feedstock 22.1 and fuel 7.2–9.0 in BREF Table 2.6 for conventional reforming, a reported level and not BAT. The IEA table (Chapter 1) gives BAT values of 21.0 feedstock, 11.1 fuel and 0.3 electricity, with gross 32.4 and net 27.6 after 4.8 of steam; it states no boundary, steam reference or electricity convention, and that its electricity is counted as delivered is our inference. BREF Section 2.2.3.8 states 20–30% per pass without a basis; because the same section describes the synthesis gas as containing inerts, we take the total-gas value of 25.5% as the closer match (30.2% on $\mathrm { H _ { 2 } + N _ { 2 } }$ , slightly above the range, is an alternative). The converter is an equilibrium-approach model, so this value is not evidence of kinetic performance.

Further boundary efects. BREF Section 2.3.1.1 warns that energy comparisons without knowledge of design and evaluation criteria can be misleading. Delivery as liquid at $- 3 3 ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ needs an extra $0 . 3 \ \mathrm { G J / t }$ relative to liquid at ambient temperature, and raising the cooling water from 20 to $3 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ adds $0 . 7 \ : \mathrm { G J / t }$ . This design delivers refrigerated liquid with cooling water from 25 to $3 5 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ We did not correct for these efects. Further, the model’s furnace assumes complete combustion and a casing loss of 1.5% of the fired duty, its steam and refrigeration systems are not simulated (Section S8), no check flagged the energy results, and a reviewer agent called the gas use plausible.

Electricity conversion. Net energy is $2 8 . 0 0 8 + 2 . 1 1 9 / \eta - 3 . 1 6 1$ GJ/t for the design and $2 1 . 0 + 1 1 . 1 + 0 . 3 / \eta - 4 . 8$ for the IEA value: at $\eta = 1 0 0 \% , 2 6 . 9 7$ and 27.60; at 50%, 29.09 and 27.90; at 40%, 30.14 and 28.05. Neither source states η, so 40% is our assumption. The design crosses the lower BREF bound at $\eta = 7 7 \%$ . The BREF range also carries a stated 1.5 GJ. Direct $\mathrm { C O _ { 2 } }$ with all purchased power at the IEA’s global average of 475 $\mathrm { g / k W h }$ is $1 . 5 5 8 + 0 . 5 8 9 \times 0 . 4 7 5 = 1 . 8 4 ~ \mathrm { t / t }$

Limits of the blinding. One design job (run 20261029-232421-s5b-implement), whose task told it to find citable data, searched for and downloaded the BREF during the run. Its transcript, which is not published because it may hold third-party text, shows that it saw 27.6–31.8, 22.1 and 7.2–9.0, 100–250 bar and 20–30%. It recorded only 22.1 and 7.2–9.0 in DESIGN.md D-034, as a check on the design. The job computing the indicators saw that line when searching the design files and reports not using it; its values come from the script. Gas use was already about 27.8 GJ/t before the search; it later became 28.01 GJ/t through the feed specification approved by the author (D-040). We found no other design value moved toward these figures but cannot exclude influence. The reference values were selected by the orchestrating session, the same model as the designers.

Agent-driven change: what it did. The session worked in four iterations. Agent-driven change: what it reported. These values are from the agent’s own log and no engineer has reviewed them: product 1,799.2 t/d; natural gas 0.5706 t/t NH<sub>3</sub>; electricity 0.589 MWh/t (44.1 MW); steam export 1.02 $\mathrm { t / t } ;$ carbon footprint 1.465 kg $\mathrm { C O _ { 2 } - e q / k g ; }$ equipment cost USD 362.0 to 404.4 million; levelized cost 688 to 674 EUR/t. The levelized cost falls because the equipment cost rises by 12%, not 20%: items priced on duty or power were repriced, but vessels and columns kept their 1,500 t/d dimensions, which the agent noted understates the cost.

Agent-driven change: what stayed open. The agent judged that every throughput-sized item must be resized or re-rated, and it did not do this. Its list covers machines and motors, the H-101 furnace and R-101 tubes, reactors and catalyst beds at the same space velocity, every exchanger, columns and separators, the steam and cooling water systems, and relief devices. By its own screening, 36 gas lines exceed the design velocities, 20 of them by more than 10%. Equipment dimensions and weights, and with them the layout, MTO and vessel and column costs, remain those of the 1,500 t/d plant. It stated that it would not trust the costs and the levelized cost, the exchanger areas (scaled with duty at unchanged temperature diference), or the relief loads (rated flows without machine curves, with no device sizes calculated). It also noted that higher pressure drops, of-design machines and a diferent loop pressure are not modeled. The HAZOP study was left for a team to reassess.
