# Beyond Accuracy: Disentangling Decomposition from Typed-State Quality Assurance in Multi-Agent LLM Pipelines

Maria Gabriela Alves Zuppardo Centro de Informática, Universidade Federal de Pernambuco (UFPE) Recife, PE, Brazil mgaz@cin.ufpe.br

Vinicius Cardoso Garcia Centro de Informática, Universidade Federal de Pernambuco (UFPE) Recife, PE, Brazil vcg@cin.ufpe.br

## ABSTRACT

Multi-agent LLM pipelines are often credited with gains in software engineering tasks, but it is rarely clear whether a gain stems from the typed contract between agents or from the specialized prompts that decomposition enables. We study this attribution problem in Requirements Engineering, comparing three architectures for classifying requirements as functional or non-functional and then prioritizing them with MoSCoW: a single-call baseline; a two-call variant that reuses the pipeline’s specialized prompts but passes an unvalidated intermediate; and a pipeline with a Pydanticvalidated typed state. The 18 conditions cover PROMISE NFR+ (625 requirements), three locally hosted small language models, and two languages. The F1 gain over the baseline is associated with the two-call, specialized-prompt variant (up to +0.075), whereas the typed state adds a small diference (−0.003 to +0.013) that is significant in one of six comparisons. Beyond accuracy, the typed pipeline contains out-of-range model outputs at the agent bound ary, while the unvalidated variant fails later and drops the requirement, and it shows a small, reproducible increase in inter-model MoSCoW consistency whose cause our design cannot isolate. The study is associative: the contrasts bundle prompts, call count, and staged context, and the results come from three 7–8B models on one dataset. The implementation and replication package are archived on Zenodo (https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23380444).

## 1 Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed as components of larger software pipelines, where their outputs feed downstream decisions with limited human review [10]. This raises a quality assurance question that has received little controlled empirical attention in the Brazilian Software Quality community: when a pipeline is decomposed into specialised agents connected by a typed contract, what does that contract buy beyond the accuracy of the underlying model calls? Recent SBQS work shares the evaluatebeyond-accuracy concern in adjacent settings, such as judging LLM-generated unit tests against mutation-testing criteria [1] and assessing generative AI as a substitute for usability inspectors [2]. Practitioners likewise cite reliability and weak contextual grounding as primary barriers to adopting LLMs in Requirements Engineering [14].

None of the studies we surveyed isolates the contribution of an architectural mechanism, such as a typed inter-agent contract, from that of prompt design. When a pipeline outperforms a singleagent baseline, the gain is often read as evidence that the contract improves output quality; whether prompting alone would produce the same gain is rarely checked, and systematic reviews confirm that this conflation remains unresolved [6, 18] (§3). F1 is not this paper’s object of interest but the instrument we use to rule out one explanation before asking a more specific question: we show that the typed contract barely moves F1 $( \Delta _ { s } \approx 0 )$ , which points to decomposition and its task-specific prompts as the accuracy driver and leaves open what diferences accompany the typed contract, a question F1 cannot answer (§6.5, §6.6).

We therefore ask not only whether a multi-agent pipeline refines classification accuracy, but which architectural element drives any gain and what diferences accompany the typed contract. We operationalise quality assurance narrowly, as early structural validation and cross-model behavioural consistency, loosely related to ISO/IEC 25010’s Reliability (we do not measure Maintainability) rather than the Functional Suitability that F1 already captures. We compare three architectures on FR/NFR classification followed by MoSCoW prioritisation, using PROMISE NFR+ [15, 16] (625 requirements): a baseline that issues a single LLM call per requirement; a pipeline of two specialised agents orchestrated with LangGraph [9] over a Pydantic-validated typed state; and a two-call baseline that reuses the pipeline’s task-specific prompts but passes an unvalidated intermediate object. Because the second call also receives the first call’s decided type and category, the baseline-to-two-call diference is a decomposition efect that bundles prompt wording, call count, and staged context (§4.5, §6.5). All three run on the same three locally served SLMs and decoding settings, yielding an 18-condition grid (3 models × 3 architectures × 2 languages).

Here, “disentangling” refers to comparing architectural contrasts; it does not imply causal isolation of the typed contract’s efect.

## 2 Background

LLM-based agents. LLMs have evolved from passive text generators into agents that invoke tools, maintain state, and work with other agents [10]. Multi-agent systems compose such agents into pipelines in which each handles a sub-task and communicates through typed messages or shared state, reflecting classical design principles (task specialisation, typed contracts, composability). Their empirical benefit remains open, since shared context may ofset the gains of decomposition, and it splits into two facets that this paper treats separately: functional accuracy, and quality attributes (e.g., ISO/IEC 25010 Reliability) that an accuracy metric does not capture.

RE and NFR classification. PROMISE NFR+ [15, 16] provides 625 requirements across one functional class and ten NFR categories, the canonical dataset for controlled classification studies in the Brazilian community [11]. MoSCoW prioritisation [3] (Must, Should, Could, Won’t Have) lacks a public ground truth, which motivates treating MoSCoW consistency as a quality proxy rather than an accuracy metric (§6.3).

Small language models. SLMs (≤ 8B parameters) are competi tive on SE classification tasks [13, 19], and local deployment enables controlled, versioned experiments. Structured-output compliance, however, varies across SLMs [7], a failure mode that matters inside pipelines, where a malformed output at one agent’s boundary can spread into the next agent’s context (§6.6).

## 3 Related Work

Recent work has investigated the use of Large Language Models (LLMs) in Requirements Engineering (RE) for tasks such as the classification of functional and non-functional requirements (FR/NFR), prioritization, traceability, and requirements generation. However, most studies evaluate the models as standalone predictors and emphasize classification accuracy, paying less attention to the architectural mechanisms that coordinate multiple interactions between LLMs. FR/NFR classification has been extensively investigated using the PROMISE NFR+ benchmark [15, 16]. Recent studies compare proprietary and open-source models under various prompting strategies [11], combine fine-tuned Small Language Models (SLMs) with explainability techniques [13], and analyze performance dif ferences between compact and proprietary models [19]. In general, these works aim to improve predictive performance while keeping the underlying processing architecture unchanged. From a broader perspective, systematic reviews by Zadenoori et al. [18] and Hemmat et al. [6] identify growing interest in the use of LLMs in RE but also point out a lack of rigorous, reproducible evaluations of architectural design decisions. Concurrently, LLM-based multi-agent systems are gaining traction in software engineering tasks, driven by the decomposition of work among specialized agents and explicit orchestration mechanisms [10]. Although existing studies often report end-to-end performance gains, few isolate the efects of decomposition and the architectural decisions governing agent interaction. This work addresses this attribution problem through a controlled experimental design. We compare three configurations: a single-call baseline, a multi-agent pipeline with typed contracts between agents, and a two-call baseline without typed state. By keeping the underlying models and decoding configurations constant, the design makes it possible to distinguish the efects of task-specific decomposition and prompting from those associated with the typed contract between agents. The gap we claim is relative to the two systematic reviews above and to SBQS/SBES proceedings of 2024–2025; our survey was not systematic.

## 4 Implementation and Experimental Platform

All three architectures address the same compound RE task, FR/NFR classification followed by MoSCoW prioritisation, and share the dataset adapter, model factory, decoding parameters, retry policy, concurrency level, and metric computation. As Table 1 shows, they difer in more than the intermediate representation; we state each diference so that efects are not attributed to a single factor (§4.5).

## 4.1 Architectural Overview

The baseline classifies and prioritises in a single LLM call (BaselineAgent, joint prompt, no intermediate state). The two-call baseline (TwoCallBaselineAgent) uses the same task-specific prompt builders as the pipeline but passes the intermediate result in an unvalidated Python dataclass, so Pydantic validation happens only when the final output is assembled. The pipeline divides the work between two agents that exchange a validated Pydantic object, orchestrated by LangGraph. In both two-call arms the prioritisation prompt receives the type and category decided by the first call.

Comparing the baseline with the two-call baseline bundles decomposition, call count, task-specific prompts, and staged context. Comparing the two-call baseline with the pipeline bundles the typed contract, the validation boundary, the handling of confidence, and LangGraph orchestration. Neither comparison isolates a single component.

## 4.2 Design and Pipeline

The code follows a layered structure with a one-way dependency rule (presentation, application, domain, infrastructure; the domain layer imports from none of the others). Four patterns carry the experimental control: an Abstract Factory (build\_llm) selects the provider by model-name prefix; an Adapter (PromiseAdapter) isolates the dataset schema from the prompts; a Template Method (BaseAgent[Item, Output]) fixes the agent contract; and a Strategy (OrchestrationStrategy) lets one runner execute every architecture from the same configuration. Prompts are versioned files whose version is recorded in each manifest.

The pipeline consists of ClassificationAgent and PrioritizationAgent. Agents process requirements in a pool of three workers, retry each call up to three times with exponential back-of, and preserve input order; the global rank is recomputed by sorting on priority\_score. The prioritisation prompt receives the classifier’s confidence and, below 0.70, instructs the model to be conservative; the threshold is a design choice, not a calibrated parameter. The workflow is a LangGraph StateGraph with the fixed topology classifier → prioritizer → END; only the pipeline uses LangGraph.

The agents share a PipelineState (a Pydantic BaseModel) whose requirement entity grows by inheritance (Requirement → ClassifiedRequirement → PrioritizedRequirement). Agent outputs have bounded fields: confidence and priority\_score lie in [0, 1], priority\_rank is at least 1, and the requirement type and MoSCoW label are enumerations. A value outside these bounds raises a ValidationError when the typed object is built, inside the agent and immediately after the call; validation is thus a property of these constructors, not of LangGraph’s state assignment. The agent’s parser catches the error and substitutes a flagged fallback (§4.3).

## 4.3 Robustness Layer

Small models frequently return malformed or of-schema JSON. Every agent extracts the outermost JSON object with a regular expression (duplicated in each agent rather than centralised). The classification-producing agents additionally normalise the category (mapping Portuguese or English prose to the PROMISE code) and repair a recurrent of-schema pattern in which the model returns an NFR category code (e.g., PE) in the requirement-type field, reading it as NF. The repair was needed only by mistral:7b, in 16 of 625 PT and 7 of 625 EN outputs, with identical counts in the pipeline classifier and the two-call baseline. If parsing or validation still fails, the agent returns a deterministic fallback instead of raising: FUNCTIONAL with confidence zero for classification and COULD\_HAVE with score 0.5 for prioritisation, so a batch is never aborted. Fallbacks are identifiable (confidence zero and an error string) and are the outputs excluded or retained in the sensitivity analysis (§5.6).

## 4.4 Execution, Traceability, and Verification

One ExperimentRunner executes every architecture from a frozen configuration and writes a manifest (strategy, model, language, seed, temperature, prompt version, dataset path and MD5 hash, LangGraph version, elapsed time, code commit) and a results file with the metrics and every prediction. Per-call JSONL traces were retained only for the two-call supplement runs. The main grid has 12 conditions: six for the baseline and six for the pipeline (three models, two languages); the two-call ablation adds six, for 18 in total.

The unit tests cover agent parsing, category normalisation and fallbacks, the domain models, the runner and manifest, the statistical routines, the language rule (a test fails if any agent sends Portuguese text in an English condition), and the confidence rule (the pipeline’s prioritiser receives the classifier’s confidence; the two-call baseline fixes it at 1.0). A fault-injection campaign injects 13 faults (six value-range violations, e.g. confidence 1.4 or priority\_score −0.1, and seven parse-level faults); the tests assert that the typed pipeline contains each value-range violation at the agent boundary, whereas the unvalidated intermediate fails only when the output model is assembled (§6.6).

## 4.5 Known Limitations of the Implementation

Five aspects bound what the experiments can attribute. First, confidence-aware routing is wired but almost never fires: only three of 3,750 pipeline predictions (two for qwen2.5:7b PT, one for mistral:7b EN, all parse fallbacks excluded from the metrics) have confidence below 0.70, so its isolated efect cannot be evaluated; the two-call baseline fixes the confidence at 1.0. Second, response parsing is regex-based and duplicated across agents, so a response with several brace-delimited blocks fails extraction and becomes a fallback. Third, seed governs dataset sampling only and is not forwarded to the model server, so determinism rests on temperature 0. Fourth, agents write their results into the shared state object in place. Fifth, we evaluated the design only in RE, although we expect the principles to apply to other multi-stage SE tasks.

## 5 Methodology

## 5.1 Research Questions

Two research questions guide this study, complemented by a language-efect analysis (§6.2) that is reported alongside the RQs but is not itself a formal RQ, since it varies text language and prompt language jointly rather than isolating either as a controlled factor.

RQ1. To what extent does the pipeline outperform the singleagent baseline in FR/NFR binary classification? We report Macro F1 and test the paired per-requirement outcomes across the six model–language combinations with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (confidence-zero fallbacks excluded) and the exact McNemar test (fallbacks retained), with Bonferroni correction $( \alpha ^ { \prime } = 0 . 0 5 / 6 =$ 0.0083).

RQ2 (central). To what extent does decomposition with its taskspecific prompts, rather than the typed inter-agent state contract, account for the F1 diference between the pipeline and the single agent baseline, and what diferences accompany the typed state contract that F1 does not capture? We examine this through two architectural contrasts, $\Delta _ { \mathrm { p } } = \mathrm { F } 1 ( \mathrm { t w o - c a l l } ) - \mathrm { F } 1 ( \mathrm { b a s e l i n e } )$ and $\Delta _ { s } = $ F1(pipeline) − F1(two-call). The first bundles task-specific prompts, an additional call, and staged context. The second bundles the typed Pydantic state, its validation boundary, confidence-conditioned prompting (which fired for only 3 of 3,750 pipeline predictions, §4.5), and LangGraph orchestration. Neither contrast isolates a single mechanism, and because the unified baseline prompt and the task-specific prompts cannot be made strictly identical (§7), Δ is read as an upper bound on the F1 diference associated with the typed state rather than as a point estimate. Validation and error propagation behaviour are examined separately (§6.5, §6.6).

Inter-model MoSCoW consistency is reported as a software quality observation (Fleiss’ � with a paired bootstrap of Δ�; §6.3) rather than as accuracy, because PROMISE NFR+ provides no priority ground truth.

Complementary analysis: language efect. We compare an English condition (original text and English prompt) with a Portuguese condition (translated text and Portuguese prompt) across all three architectures. Because text and prompt language change together, the results capture their joint efect rather than either in isolation; the residual confound is acknowledged in §7 (§6.2).

## 5.2 Dataset

We use PROMISE NFR+ [15, 16], the canonical benchmark for requirements classification, comprising 625 requirements: 255 functional (F) and 370 non-functional (NF) across 10 categories (A, FT, LF, MN, O, PE, PO, SC, SE, US).

The original dataset is in English. To support the language-efect analysis, we produced a Portuguese variant by machine-translating each requirement with deep\_translator (Google Translate, 2024). Both variants share identical ground-truth labels. We checked translation quality via back-translation (PT→EN, BERTScore F1 with roberta-large): the mean score was 0.9646 across all 625 pairs, and 624 of 625 scored ≥ 0.90. The single below-threshold item was manually inspected and confirmed semantically equivalent.

## 5.3 Architectural Variants

The architectural variant is the primary independent variable for RQ1: baseline, pipeline, and two-call baseline (§4) all accept the same 625 requirements and produce outputs of identical structure, so that architecture is the main manipulated factor (the remaining implementation diferences are listed in Table 1 and §4.5). Their mechanics are described in §4.1–4.2; in brief, the baseline issues one unified LLM call, the pipeline decomposes the task via a typed PipelineState, and the two-call baseline reuses the pipeline’s prompts without the typed contract.

## 6 Results

We report RQ1 (§6.1), the language-efect analysis (§6.2), MoSCoW consistency (§6.3), NFR subcategory diagnostics (§6.4), RQ2 (§6.5), and validation timing (§6.6).

In short, most of the pipeline’s F1 gain is associated with the two-call variant with specialised prompts rather than with the typed state, which is accompanied by earlier containment of out-of-range values and a small, unisolated consistency diference.

Uniform exclusion policy. All accuracy, F1, and MCC figures are computed after excluding confidence-zero parse-failure fallbacks (38 outputs across 4 of the 18 conditions), which are infrastructure artefacts of the shared parsing layer rather than model predictions, applied identically to all conditions (§6.6 details which are afected). Because exclusion is an analyst’s choice, we also report the reading that retains them as incorrect predictions: McNemar tests and Cohen’s � are computed on all 623 aligned pairs, and the Wilcoxon sensitivity is stated wherever it changes a conclusion. Exclusion is the primary analysis because it isolates model behaviour; the retained reading is the end-to-end sensitivity analysis, in which a parsing failure is an unsuccessful system outcome, and it is reported in full beside it. ## 6.6 Validation Timing and Schema Compliance

The clearest evidence concerning the typed state’s qualityassurance properties is a diference in when structural validation occurs, not a metric. In all three architectures, a parse or enum-conversion failure is caught inside the responsible agent’s \_parse\_response and replaced with a conservative default (FUNCTIONAL, confidence=0.0); no malformed value reaches a downstream agent’s context. This robustness is not attributable to the typed contract: all four agents use the same regex-based JSON extraction and fallback, duplicated across the codebase.

The typed contract’s verifiable contribution is validation timing. In the pipeline, ClassificationOutput’s Pydantic constraints (e.g., confidence in [0, 1]) are enforced immediately after the ClassificationAgent call, before the result enters PipelineState. In the two-call ablation the intermediate is a plain @dataclass, and Pydantic validation occurs only when the final output is assembled after the second call. The typed contract thus validates one LLM call earlier, a narrow V&V property: a violation could be acted on before the second, more expensive call.

On the PROMISE NFR+ grid this diference never surfaced as a failure: no confidence or type value fell outside its bounds in any of the 18 runs. Parse-failure fallbacks are rare: 38 outputs across 4 runs (33 baseline llama3.1:8b EN, 5.3%; 2 pipeline qwen2.5:7b PT; 1 pipeline mistral:7b EN; 2 two-call mistral:7b EN). We exclude them from Tables 4, 6, and 9 and report the retained reading as a sensitivity analysis (§6). The zero-confidence count is a poor proxy for structured-output compliance; a finer one is the share of predicted-NF outputs with no valid nfr\_category: for mistral:7b it is 7.3% (pipeline) and 8.1% (two-call) in Portuguese against 1.3– 1.4% in English, and no other condition exceeds 2.6%, which is what the capacity-floor reading of §6.1 relies on. The three pipeline fallbacks are the only outputs with confidence below 0.70, so only 3 of 3,750 prioritisations received the conservative-label instruction; routing was efectively inert, and no reported result depends on it.

To verify the boundary independently of the grid, we ran a systematic fault-injection campaign through the real agents of each architecture with a scripted LLM (run\_fault\_injection\_campaign.py). It injects 13 faults into the model’s JSON, in either call: six value-range violations (confidence 1.4, −0.5, NaN; priority\_score 1.5, −0.1; priority\_rank 0) and seven parse-level faults (invalid or missing enumeration, wrong type, truncated JSON). The typed contract adds nothing for parse-level faults, which the two-call baseline and the pipeline handle identically (a flagged fallback after two calls).

The diference lies in the value-range violations (Table 10). The typed pipeline rejects each where the typed object is built, detecting classification-stage faults after the first call and prioritisation-stage faults after the second, and keeps the requirement as a flagged fallback after two calls. The unvalidated two-call baseline passes the same values through its dataclass and fails when the output model is assembled, after the second call; because that failure propagates through the retry wrapper, both calls are repeated up to three attempts, consuming six calls before the requirement is dropped without a failure record. The single-call baseline contains all 13 faults at its one call, and no architecture let an out-of-range value reach the output. The campaign is synthetic: it characterises where each architecture detects a fault and what that costs, not how often such faults occur; that earlier validation prevents an operational failure remains an untested hypothesis. The six-call cost and the loss in the unvalidated arm partly reflect the shared retry policy, which retries a deterministic validation error, so the contrast is between a contained and an uncontained failure rather than between detection and non-detection.

## 7 Discussion and Threats to Validity

The experiments indicate that the typed contract is accompanied by little diference in classification performance $( \Delta _ { s } ~ \in ~ [ - 0 . 0 0 3 , + 0 . 0 1 3 ] )$ . The evidence for what it is accompanied by is limited to two properties: earlier structural validation, verified by a 13-fault injection campaign, and a statistically confirmed diference in inter-model MoSCoW consistency whose cause we did not isolate. We do not claim, because we did not test, benefits such as predictable behaviour under model substitution or auditable intermediate decisions, and even these would depend on the model satisfying the structured-output contract. For mistral:7b PT (§6.1, §6.6), parse failures fall back to FUNCTIONAL rather than NF, silently biasing misclassifications toward the functional class; since the model has no more zero-confidence outputs than the others but 5–6× more NF predictions without a valid category in Portuguese, these failures are subtler than malformed responses. Structured-output compliance is therefore a prerequisite for benefiting from decomposition.

Practically, teams interested mainly in F1 should invest in better prompts rather than a typed contract, whereas applications that value earlier structural validation or cross-model consistency may prefer the pipeline despite its limited accuracy gain over the twocall baseline. Our paired analysis does not support deploying the same architecture bilingually without adaptation (§6.2). Although the pipeline makes two sequential calls, local execution times were comparable to the baseline (qwen2.5:7b PT: 3529 s vs. 3415 s for � = 625); hosted APIs may approach a 2× wall-clock cost, which we did not test.

Internal threats. PROMISE NFR+ has no MoSCoW ground truth, so prioritisation quality is operationalised as inter-model stability, reflecting reliability rather than correctness; some requirements also admit several plausible NFR categories while the dataset gives one label. The principal threat is the prompt–architecture confound: the baseline combines both subtasks in one prompt, so $\Delta _ { \mathrm { p } }$ is a diference associated with the two-call variant, bundling prompt wording, an extra call, and staged context, and a call-countcontrolled arm would be needed to separate them. $\Delta _ { s }$ likewise bundles the validation boundary, confidence handling (which fired for 3 of 3,750 predictions), and LangGraph orchestration, so it is a pipeline-versus-two-call contrast rather than an isolated typedcontract efect, and its range is an upper bound on the F1 diference associated with the typed contract. Cohen’s ℎ places all RQ1 efects below 0.20 but ignores the pairing; the paired � is medium or large where estimable, so we do not call the efects negligible. For $\Delta _ { s }$ the 4–15 discordant pairs are too few for any reliable paired efect size. Excluding 38 fallbacks is an analyst’s decision, hence both readings; it changes one RQ1 verdict (llama3.1:8b EN) and one $\Delta _ { \mathrm { p } }$ estimate. Decoding at temperature 0 is not bit-deterministic: primary results come from one execution per condition, and the one repeated execution (pipeline and two-call) ran on a newer Ollama release (0.35.1 vs. 0.24.0), so its variation mixes run-to-run and server-version efects; diferences below about one point of F1 (and 0.03 in �) should not be interpreted. The fault-injection evidence rests on 13 synthetic faults (six of them value-range violations, which extend the earlier five-value probe): it shows where validation occurs and what it costs, not how often such faults arise. Per-call traces were retained only for the two-call supplement runs, which limits audit of individual failures.

External threats. All experiments use PROMISE NFR+ and three decoder-only SLMs (7–8B parameters) served through versioned Ollama tags, whose behaviour may change; compliance rates and the contribution oftyped contracts may difer for other datasets, domains, or models, and the � gain reflects only this three-model panel. The single larger model (§6.5) is one model with one execution per condition. The language comparison varies requirement and prompt language jointly, and the Portuguese text is a machine translation (back-translation BERTScore F1 0.9646; 624 of 625 pairs ≥ 0.90), so translation artefacts may contribute to the PT–EN gap. Only the pipeline used LangGraph, although the typed-state pattern is not specific to it. Bonferroni correction was applied throughout, and categories with fewer than 30 instances were excluded from the primary analyses. Our conclusions are therefore intentionally narrow: for the evaluated dataset, task, and model panel, the measurable diferences that accompany the typed contract are earlier structural validation and a small diference in inter-model consistency, rather than improvements in classification accuracy.

## 8 Conclusion and Future Work

In the evaluated setting, the accuracy diference is associated with decomposition into specialised prompts, not with the typed state (§6.1–§6.5). The typed state is accompanied by two diferences beyond accuracy: containment of out-of-range values at the agent boundary, with classification faults detected one call earlier in a 13-fault campaign (§6.6), and a small, reproducible increase in inter-model MoSCoW consistency (paired bootstrap, � < 0.001 in both languages, §6.3). The design does not isolate their causes, and the limits are detailed in §7.

Future Work. (i) Calibrating the confidence threshold, to isolate routing from validation timing. (ii) A call-count-controlled arm to separate prompt wording from the extra call and staged context. (iii) Larger models and a larger rater panel, to test whether Δ<sub>s</sub> grows with capacity and the consistency gain generalises.
